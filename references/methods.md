# Selecting methods for creative inquiry

Use this reference to frame a question, choose methods, or prepare research involving people or practice. These method families are a practical synthesis for agents, not an exhaustive worldwide catalog.

## Match method to uncertainty

| Uncertainty | Useful approach | What the result supports | Common overreach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What happened, existed, or was documented? | Archival and historical inquiry; primary documents; comparison of accounts | Contextualized records and a chronology with gaps | Inventing motives or assuming a surviving archive represents everyone |
| What is already known? | Desk research; literature and precedent review | Existing knowledge, disagreement, and unanswered questions | Treating search rankings or article counts as consensus |
| How does a reference work? | Close analysis of the available work; comparison with relevant examples | Relationships among form, content, sequence, and medium | Certifying intention or audience effect from the artifact alone |
| What do people experience or do? | Interviews; contextual observation; diaries or photojournals | Situated accounts, actions, and context | Treating self-report as observed behavior or a few accounts as population prevalence |
| What meanings or unexpected possibilities might emerge? | Cultural probes; open creative tasks; reflective interpretation | Evocative material and provisional readings | Converting ambiguous responses into definitive user requirements |
| What should be explored with a community? | Participatory inquiry; co-design; participatory prototyping | Shared questions, alternatives, and local knowledge | Calling a feedback request shared authorship or claiming consensus without it |
| Where else does this mechanism occur? | Analogous inspiration | A transferable relationship and a proposed adaptation | Assuming the original context proves the adaptation will work |
| What can a material or medium do? | Tinkering; samples; rehearsals; technical and experiential investigation | Observed behavior under recorded conditions and new possibilities | Claiming sensory or mechanical results without the relevant access |
| What happens if we realize an idea? | Research through making; prototype comparison; documented iteration | Lessons from actual artifacts and their development | Calling every production iteration a general research contribution |
| How is the work received? | Contextual feedback; comprehension or experience studies; appropriate experiments | Responses under the study's conditions | Predicting broad impact from preference, politeness, or self-review |
| Which assumptions deserve questioning? | Critical/speculative proposals; scenarios and counterfactuals | Exposed assumptions and possible alternatives | Presenting a scenario as a forecast or fiction as testimony |

Combine methods when they answer complementary questions. Archives establish documented details, practitioner accounts explain current practice, and a scene study explores how selected details function in a film. These results have different evidential roles.

## Frame without flattening the work

Name the subject and boundary: whose experience, which place or tradition, what period, which medium, and which decision. Inspect the user's starting material before redefining the task. Avoid leading questions such as how to prove that a preferred style is best. Investigate the relevant effect or association instead.

For exploratory art, a starting point may be an unresolved relation: memory and repetition, a gesture and its resistance, or a sound and the space it inhabits. Let practice refine the question. Do not impose a metric or fixed hypothesis where interpretive exploration is the purpose.

Specify what would count as useful learning. A historical detail needs an attributable record; a new movement phrase needs rehearsal; a claim about comprehension needs people encountering the work. Research effort follows the decision's uncertainty and consequence, not a fixed number of sources.

## Prepare inquiries involving people

Select actual or relevant participants for the question. Describe who is included and which experiences are missing. Sample size, recruitment, and representativeness depend on the study; do not prescribe a universal quota or infer statistical prevalence from a qualitative sample.

For interviews, ask open questions about concrete experiences before introducing your concept. Preserve the basis for quotations and distinguish recollection from independently documented events. Ask for examples or a walkthrough where useful. Observe context only when accessible; a text transcript cannot establish body language.

For observation, record the setting, time, activity, and what was available to the observer. Keep interpretation separate. An occasional visit is bounded contextual observation; do not claim comprehensive ethnography from it.

For diaries and photojournals, let participants describe their selections and omissions. Avoid guessing a photograph's meaning from appearance. For cultural probes, retain ambiguity when the activity is designed for evocative responses; choose a different method if the task needs specific factual answers.

In participatory work, make the role concrete: participants help frame the question, create alternatives, interpret findings, or shape the test. State who makes the final decision. Plan suitable participation, including language, sensory, mobility, or technology needs relevant to the activity.

Before collecting or recording participant material, explain the purpose, use, audience, and voluntary nature of participation. Use material within the agreed scope. Planning an interview does not authorize contacting people; conducting one requires actual authorization and participation. See [evidence-and-context.md](evidence-and-context.md) for handling the material.

## Understand practice-related approaches

Candy and Edmonds distinguish practice-based research, where a creative artifact is central to the contribution, from practice-led research, whose contribution primarily concerns understanding practice. Terminology varies across disciplines. For academic research, follow the relevant institution or field rather than claiming these labels settle the question.

Research through design can use making to investigate under-constrained questions. Zimmerman and colleagues' model is situated in interaction design/HCI; using it elsewhere is an adaptation. For an ordinary creative project, a documented experiment may inform that work without claiming a scholarly contribution.

Formal R&D classification serves a different purpose from everyday project research. The OECD Frascati Manual uses novelty, creativity, uncertainty, systematic work, and transferability/reproducibility as criteria. Do not require every creative investigation to qualify as R&D or describe a routine moodboard as qualifying merely because it is creative.

## Sources and scope

- [IDEO.org: Secondary Research](https://www.designkit.org/methods/secondary-research.html), [Interview](https://www.designkit.org/methods/interview.html), [Immersion](https://www.designkit.org/methods/immersion.html), [Photojournal](https://www.designkit.org/methods/photojournal.html), [Analogous Inspiration](https://www.designkit.org/methods/analogous-inspiration.html), and [Participatory Prototyping](https://www.designkit.org/methods/participatory-prototyping.html): practical human-centered methods. Suggested schedules and counts are contextual examples, not universal requirements here.
- [Gaver and colleagues: Cultural Probes and the Value of Uncertainty, 2004](https://feast.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2019/07/cultural-probe.pdf): probes as evocative, subjective material; preserve the distinction from requirements gathering.
- [Candy and Edmonds: Practice-Based Research in the Creative Arts, 2018](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/rest/bitstreams/1b90707e-ba48-4cb7-89e8-5c281ecbb63c/retrieve): practice, artifacts, reflection, and scrutinizable research contributions.
- [Zimmerman, Forlizzi, and Evenson: Research Through Design, 2007](https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i262/s13/readings_pdf/Zimmerman_Research_Through_Design_as_a_Method_for_IaD_in_HCI_0.pdf): a model developed for interaction design research in HCI.
- [Dunne and Raby: Critical Design FAQ](https://dunneandraby.co.uk/content/bydandr/13/0): speculative proposals that challenge assumptions; a position rather than a fixed recipe.
- [OECD: Frascati Manual 2015](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2015/10/frascati-manual-2015_g1g57dcb/9789264239012-en.pdf): criteria for classifying R&D.

These entry points do not represent every research tradition. For a named local practice, investigate its own practitioners, language, institutions, and accounts before adapting a method.
